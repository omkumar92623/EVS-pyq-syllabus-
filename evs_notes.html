Unit 1: Environmental Awareness, Ecology and Environment

Multidisciplinary Nature of Environmental Science 10 Marks PYQ

Environmental science is not a single, isolated subject but a highly complex, multidisciplinary field. It requires the integration of multiple academic disciplines to understand the natural world and solve environmental problems.

  • Life Sciences (Botany, Zoology, Microbiology): Used to study biodiversity, ecosystems, and the biological impact of pollutants on plants and animals.
  • Physical Sciences (Chemistry, Physics): Essential for understanding the chemical structure of pollutants, atmospheric thermodynamics, and energy flow in ecosystems.
  • Earth Sciences (Geology, Geography, Oceanography): Help in studying soil erosion, groundwater depletion, and ocean currents.
  • Social Sciences (Sociology, Economics): Address the human aspects of environmental issues, such as population explosion, cost-benefit analysis of conservation, and human behavior.
  • Mathematics & Computer Science: Used for statistical analysis of environmental data, climate modeling, and predicting future environmental scenarios.

Example: To address a polluted river, we need Chemistry to test the water, Biology to see the effect on fish, Engineering to build a treatment plant, and Law to enforce regulations. Thus, it is inherently multidisciplinary.

Definition, Scope, and Importance 3 Marks PYQ

Definition: Environmental Science is the systematic, scientific study of the environment, its living and non-living components, and the interactions between them.

Scope: It encompasses natural resource management, ecology, biodiversity conservation, environmental pollution control, and social issues connected to the environment.

Importance: It is crucial for achieving sustainable development (meeting present needs without compromising future generations), protecting human health from toxins, and maintaining the delicate ecological balance of the Earth.

Need for Public Awareness 10 Marks PYQ

Environmental protection cannot be achieved by laws and governments alone; it requires the active participation of every individual. Hence, public awareness is absolutely critical.

  • Why it is needed: Unrestrained human activities (wasting water, using single-use plastics, deforestation) are the root cause of environmental degradation. An aware public makes eco-friendly choices, holds corporations accountable, and supports green government policies.
  • How we can make the public aware:
    1. Formal Education: Integrating EVS into school and college curriculums (as mandated by the Supreme Court of India).
    2. Mass Media: Using newspapers, television, and radio to broadcast documentaries and environmental campaigns.
    3. Social Media & Internet: Utilizing platforms like Twitter and Instagram to run rapid awareness campaigns and mobilize youth.
    4. NGOs and Government Campaigns: Organizations conducting tree-plantation drives, and government initiatives like Swachh Bharat Abhiyan.
    5. Eco-Clubs: Forming community and student groups to actively participate in local conservation efforts.

Concept of an Ecosystem 3 Marks PYQ

The term "Ecosystem" was coined by A.G. Tansley in 1935. An ecosystem is defined as a structural and functional unit of the biosphere consisting of a community of living beings (biotic) and their physical environment (abiotic), interacting and exchanging materials with each other.

PYQ Note: Can an aquarium be addressed as a complete ecosystem?
Yes, an aquarium is a complete, albeit artificial (man-made) ecosystem. It has abiotic components (water, gravel, oxygen, light) and biotic components (fish, plants, bacteria). The plants produce oxygen, fish consume plants/food and produce waste, and bacteria decompose the waste. However, unlike natural ecosystems, it requires external human intervention (feeding, cleaning) to remain stable.

Structure and Function of an Ecosystem 10 Marks PYQ

1. Structure of an Ecosystem: The structure is divided into two main components:

  • Abiotic (Non-living) Components: Includes climatic factors (temperature, light, humidity) and edaphic/chemical factors (soil, water, pH, minerals like Carbon and Nitrogen).
  • Biotic (Living) Components:
    • Producers (Autotrophs): Green plants and algae that manufacture their own food using sunlight via photosynthesis.
    • Consumers (Heterotrophs): Animals that cannot make their own food. Divided into Primary (Herbivores), Secondary (Carnivores eating herbivores), and Tertiary (Top carnivores).
    • Decomposers (Saprotrophs): Microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) that break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrients back into the soil.

2. Function of an Ecosystem: The ecosystem functions through two major, distinct processes:

  • Energy Flow: The transfer of energy from the sun to producers, and then through various consumers. This flow is always unidirectional.
  • Biogeochemical Cycling: The cyclic flow of nutrients (like Carbon, Nitrogen, Water) from the abiotic environment to living organisms and back again.

Examples of Specific Ecosystems (from PYQs):

  • Forest Ecosystem: Characterized by a dominance of trees. Has a complex structure with different layers (canopy, understory, forest floor). Functions to regulate climate, prevent soil erosion, and house immense biodiversity (e.g., deer as primary consumers, tigers as tertiary consumers).
  • Aquatic Ecosystem: Encompasses water-based environments. Divided into Marine (oceans, high salt) and Freshwater (lakes, rivers, ponds). In a freshwater ecosystem, phytoplankton act as producers, zooplankton and small fish as primary consumers, and large fish as secondary consumers.

Energy Flow in an Ecosystem (Ecosystem Energetics) 10 Marks PYQ

Ecosystem energetics refers to the study of the flow of energy through the trophic levels of an ecosystem.

  • Unidirectional Flow: Energy enters the ecosystem as sunlight captured by producers. It then flows to herbivores, then carnivores. Energy NEVER flows backward from consumers to producers.
  • Lindeman’s 10% Law: Only about 10% of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level. The remaining 90% is lost to the environment as heat during respiration and metabolic processes.
  • Because of this massive loss of energy at each step, food chains rarely have more than 4 or 5 trophic levels.

Biogeochemical Cycles 10 Marks PYQ

Biogeochemical cycles refer to the continuous, cyclic movement of nutrient elements between the living (bio) and non-living (geo) components of the biosphere.

1. Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is essential for proteins and DNA, but plants cannot use atmospheric Nitrogen (N2) directly. The cycle involves:

  • Nitrogen Fixation: Conversion of atmospheric N2 into ammonia by lightning or symbiotic bacteria (like Rhizobium in legume roots).
  • Nitrification: Soil bacteria convert ammonia into nitrites, and then into nitrates (the form plants can absorb).
  • Assimilation: Plants absorb nitrates from the soil to build proteins; animals get nitrogen by eating plants.
  • Ammonification: Decomposers break down dead plants and animals, turning their nitrogen back into ammonia.
  • Denitrification: Denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soil convert nitrates back into N2 gas, releasing it into the atmosphere.

2. Carbon Cycle

Carbon is the backbone of all organic molecules.

  • Photosynthesis: Plants pull CO2 from the atmosphere to make glucose.
  • Respiration: Plants and animals break down glucose for energy, releasing CO2 back into the air.
  • Decomposition: Dead organisms decay, slowly releasing carbon into the soil. Over millions of years, this can form fossil fuels.
  • Combustion: Burning of fossil fuels by humans artificially releases massive amounts of trapped carbon back into the atmosphere as CO2, driving global warming.

3. Phosphorous Cycle & Global Budget

Unlike Carbon and Nitrogen, Phosphorous is a sedimentary cycle; it has no gaseous/atmospheric phase. It is a slow cycle.

  • Weathering: Phosphorous is locked in rocks. Rain and weathering slowly release phosphate ions into soil and water.
  • Assimilation & Food Chain: Plants absorb phosphates from the soil. Animals eat the plants.
  • Decomposition: When animals and plants die, decomposers return phosphates to the soil.
  • Global Budget: The global phosphorous budget is increasingly disrupted by human activity. We mine phosphorous rock extensively to create artificial fertilizers. When this fertilizer washes into water bodies (runoff), it causes severe water pollution (eutrophication).

Food Chain and Food Web 5 Marks PYQ

Food Chain Food Web
A simple, linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one eats another. (e.g., Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake) A complex, interconnected network of multiple overlapping food chains in a single ecosystem.
Highly unstable. If one organism is removed, the entire chain can collapse. Highly stable. If one prey species drops in number, predators can switch to alternate prey.

Ecological Pyramids 5 Marks PYQ

An ecological pyramid is a graphical representation of the relationship between different organisms in an ecosystem. The base always represents producers, and higher tiers represent consumers.

  • Pyramid of Energy: Represents the flow of energy. Always upright, because energy is always lost as it moves up trophic levels.
  • Pyramid of Biomass: Represents the total dry mass of living organisms. Mostly upright (forests), but inverted in aquatic ecosystems because the biomass of rapidly reproducing phytoplankton (producers) at any given time is less than the biomass of the zooplankton and fish that eat them.
  • Pyramid of Numbers: Represents the count of individual organisms. Upright in grasslands, but can be inverted (e.g., a single large tree supporting hundreds of insects).

Unit 2: Environmental Pollution

Segments of Environment Syllabus

The natural environment is divided into four interconnected spheres:

  • Lithosphere: The solid, outer part of the Earth (crust and upper mantle) containing soil and rocks.
  • Hydrosphere: All the water on Earth's surface, underground, and in the air (oceans, rivers, glaciers).
  • Atmosphere: The blanket of gases surrounding the Earth.
  • Biosphere: The narrow zone where the Lithosphere, Hydrosphere, and Atmosphere interact, making life possible.

Definition and Causes of Environmental Pollution 10 Marks PYQ

Definition: Pollution is the introduction of harmful materials (pollutants) into the environment that cause adverse changes to the natural quality of air, water, or soil, thereby negatively affecting living organisms.

Major Causes:

  • Industrialization: Unregulated factories releasing toxic chemicals, heavy metals, and smoke into water and air.
  • Urbanization: Rapid expansion of cities leading to massive generation of sewage, solid waste, and vehicular emissions.
  • Agricultural Practices: Overuse of synthetic fertilizers and chemical pesticides which wash off into rivers (non-point source pollution).
  • Deforestation: Cutting down forests reduces the Earth's ability to absorb CO2, worsening air quality and climate change.
  • Fossil Fuels: Burning coal, oil, and gas for energy and transportation is the primary driver of air pollution and global warming.

Sources, Pathways and Fate of Environmental Pollutants Syllabus

  • Sources: Where the pollutant originates (e.g., a factory pipe, a car exhaust).
  • Pathways: The route a pollutant takes through the environment (e.g., carried by wind currents, dissolved in groundwater, moving up the food chain).
  • Fate: The final resting place or chemical state of the pollutant (e.g., settling into ocean sediments, accumulating in human fatty tissue, or degrading into harmless substances).

Physical, Chemical, and Biological Transformation of Pollutants 10 Marks PYQ

Once released, pollutants do not remain static; they undergo various transformations in the environment.

  • Physical Transformation: Changes in physical state or concentration without altering the chemical structure. Examples include the dispersion of smoke by wind, dilution of toxic chemicals in a large river, or the settling of dust particles (sedimentation) onto the ground.
  • Chemical Transformation: Pollutants react with other substances in the environment to form new chemicals. For example, Sulfur Dioxide (SO2) reacts with oxygen and water vapor in the atmosphere to form Sulfuric Acid (H2SO4), resulting in acid rain. Another example is the formation of photochemical smog driven by sunlight.
  • Biological Transformation (Biotransformation): The alteration of a pollutant by living organisms, usually microbes.
    • Biodegradation: Microbes break down organic pollutants (like sewage) into harmless substances like CO2 and water.
    • Biomagnification: Some non-degradable toxic substances (like DDT or Mercury) cannot be broken down. As they move up the food chain (from plankton to fish to eagles), their concentration magnifies, becoming highly lethal to top predators.

Population Explosion 10 Marks PYQ

Definition: Population explosion refers to the sudden, rapid, and unmanageable increase in the number of human beings in a specific area.

Causes: A massive drop in the death rate due to advancements in medical science, better sanitation, eradication of epidemics, and improved agricultural yields, while the birth rate remains relatively high.

Environmental Consequences:

  • Resource Depletion: Rapid consumption of finite resources like freshwater, fossil fuels, and minerals, pushing them towards exhaustion.
  • Deforestation & Habitat Loss: Forests are cleared at alarming rates to make way for agriculture, housing, and industries, leading to massive biodiversity loss.
  • Increased Pollution: More people means more vehicles, more factories, and exponentially more solid waste and sewage, overwhelming nature's ability to clean itself.
  • Climate Change: Increased energy demands lead to higher greenhouse gas emissions, accelerating global warming.

Environment and Human Health 10 Marks PYQ

Human health is directly and inextricably linked to the quality of the environment. Environmental degradation manifests as severe public health crises.

  • Air Pollution Effects: Inhaling particulate matter (PM2.5) and toxic gases causes respiratory illnesses (asthma, chronic bronchitis), cardiovascular diseases, and lung cancer.
  • Water Pollution Effects: Consuming contaminated water leads to deadly waterborne diseases such as cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and giardiasis, which are leading causes of infant mortality in developing nations.
  • Chemical Toxins: Exposure to heavy metals causes severe damage. Mercury poisoning leads to neurological damage (Minamata disease), while Lead exposure causes cognitive impairment in children.
  • Climate Change Impacts: Global warming increases the frequency of heatwaves (causing heat strokes) and expands the geographical range of vector-borne diseases like malaria and dengue.

Human Rights, Value Education, Women and Child Welfare 3 Marks PYQ

  • Human Rights: The right to a clean, safe, and sustainable environment is increasingly recognized globally as a fundamental human right, essential for the enjoyment of the right to life and health.
  • Value Education: Education that focuses on instilling moral and ethical values regarding the environment. It teaches empathy towards nature, respect for all living things, and the importance of a sustainable lifestyle over rampant consumerism.
  • Women and Child Welfare: Environmental degradation disproportionately affects women and children in developing areas. Women often walk miles to fetch clean water and firewood, facing physical strain. Children are highly susceptible to indoor air pollution (from burning biomass for cooking) and waterborne diseases due to their developing immune systems.

Unit 3: Air Pollution

Various Segments of Atmosphere and their Significance 10 Marks PYQ

The Earth's atmosphere is divided into distinct layers based on temperature gradients:

  • Troposphere (0 - 11 km): The lowest layer where we live. It contains 80% of the atmosphere's mass and almost all of its water vapor. Significance: All weather phenomena (clouds, rain, storms) occur here. This is also where most air pollution is trapped.
  • Stratosphere (11 - 50 km): Significance: Contains the vital Ozone Layer, which absorbs 97-99% of the sun's harmful Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, protecting life on Earth. Commercial jets fly in the lower stratosphere to avoid weather turbulence.
  • Mesosphere (50 - 85 km): The coldest layer. Significance: This is where most meteors burn up upon entering the Earth's atmosphere.
  • Thermosphere (85 - 600+ km): Temperatures rise sharply here. Significance: Contains the Ionosphere, which reflects radio waves back to earth, enabling long-distance radio communication. The beautiful Auroras (Northern Lights) occur in this layer.

Classification & Types of Air Pollutants 10 Marks PYQ

Air pollutants are broadly classified into two categories based on how they form:

  • Primary Pollutants: These are emitted directly into the atmosphere from an identifiable source.
    • Carbon Monoxide (CO): Incomplete combustion of fossil fuels (vehicles). Highly toxic.
    • Sulfur Dioxide (SO2): Burning of sulfur-containing coal in power plants.
    • Nitrogen Oxides (NOx): High-temperature combustion in car engines.
    • Particulate Matter (PM10 & PM2.5): Dust, smoke, and soot. Penetrates deep into lungs.
  • Secondary Pollutants: These are NOT emitted directly. They form in the air when primary pollutants react with each other or with sunlight/water.
    • Tropospheric Ozone (O3): Formed by NOx and Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) reacting in sunlight. (Note: Ozone is good in the stratosphere, but a dangerous pollutant at ground level).
    • Peroxyacetyl Nitrate (PAN): A toxic component of photochemical smog.
    • Acid Rain (H2SO4, HNO3): Formed when SO2 and NOx react with atmospheric moisture.

Stationary and Mobile Emissions 3 Marks PYQ

  • Stationary Emissions: Fixed, non-moving sources of pollution. Examples include industrial smoke stacks, coal-fired power plants, and oil refineries. They are easier to monitor and regulate.
  • Mobile Emissions: Moving sources of pollution. Examples include cars, trucks, airplanes, trains, and ships. They are harder to control due to their widespread and moving nature, and are a major contributor to urban smog.

Photochemical Smog vs. Sulphurous Smog 10 Marks PYQ

Smog (Smoke + Fog) is a severe form of air pollution. It exists in two primary forms:

Photochemical Smog (Los Angeles Smog) Sulphurous Smog (London Smog)
Trigger: Sunlight reacting with vehicular emissions (NOx and VOCs). Trigger: Burning of coal releasing massive amounts of SO2 and smoke.
Weather conditions: Occurs in warm, dry, and sunny climates. Weather conditions: Occurs in cold, damp, and foggy winter climates.
Nature: Oxidizing in nature (contains Ozone and PAN). Nature: Reducing in nature (high concentration of SO2).
Effects: Eye irritation, respiratory distress, damage to plant leaves. Effects: Severe lung diseases, historically caused thousands of deaths (e.g., Great Smog of London, 1952).

Green House Effect and Global Warming 10 Marks PYQ

The Greenhouse Effect: A natural process where certain gases in the Earth's atmosphere (Greenhouse Gases or GHGs) trap the Sun's heat. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth. The Earth radiates heat (infrared radiation) back outwards, but GHGs absorb and re-radiate this heat, keeping the planet warm enough to sustain life.

Major GHGs: Carbon Dioxide (CO2), Methane (CH4), Nitrous Oxide (N2O), and Water vapor.

Global Warming: Human activities (burning fossil fuels, deforestation) have artificially thickened this greenhouse gas blanket. This traps excessive heat, causing a continuous rise in the Earth's average global temperature.

Consequences:

  • Melting of polar ice caps and glaciers.
  • Thermal expansion of oceans leading to sea-level rise and coastal flooding.
  • Extreme and erratic weather patterns (more intense hurricanes, severe droughts).
  • Loss of biodiversity and destruction of coral reefs (coral bleaching).

Ozone Depletion 10 Marks PYQ

The stratospheric ozone layer protects Earth by absorbing harmful Ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

Causes of Depletion: The release of man-made chemicals, primarily Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) used in refrigerants, aerosols, and ACs. In the stratosphere, UV light breaks down CFCs, releasing highly reactive Chlorine atoms. A single Chlorine atom can destroy 100,000 ozone molecules, thinning the layer (creating the "Ozone Hole" observed over Antarctica).

Threats/Effects: Increased UV radiation reaching the surface causes skin cancer, cataracts, suppresses the human immune system, damages agricultural crops, and destroys oceanic phytoplankton (the base of the marine food chain).

Sources and their Control, AQI, and CPCB 10 Marks PYQ

Control Methods: Industrial emissions are controlled using devices like Electrostatic Precipitators (to trap dust/particulates), Scrubbers (to wash out SO2), and Baghouse filters. Vehicular emissions are reduced using Catalytic Converters and shifting to EV/CNG.

Air Quality Index (AQI): A numerical scale and color-coded system used by governments to communicate to the public how polluted the air is. It translates complex pollutant data into a single number (e.g., 0-50 is Good/Green, 301-500 is Hazardous/Maroon). It helps citizens take health precautions.

Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB): The apex statutory body in India for pollution control. Role: It sets the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS), continuously monitors air quality across cities, advises the central government, and coordinates with State Pollution Control Boards to penalize violating industries.

Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 3 Marks PYQ

Enacted in 1981, this Act provides for the prevention, control, and abatement of air pollution in India. It empowers the Central and State Pollution Control Boards to declare air pollution control areas, prohibit the use of highly polluting fuels, inspect industrial premises, and set strict emission standards. Violators can face closure of their industry or imprisonment.

Acid Rain 3 Marks PYQ

Acid rain occurs when emissions of Sulfur Dioxide (SO2) and Nitrogen Oxides (NOx) react with water, oxygen, and other chemicals in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids. These acids fall to earth as rain, snow, or fog. It damages forests, acidifies lakes killing aquatic life, and corrodes buildings and monuments (like the yellowing of the Taj Mahal).

Unit 4: Water Pollution

Water Resources Syllabus

Earth is covered by 71% water, but 97% of it is salty ocean water. Only about 3% is freshwater, and most of that is locked in ice caps and glaciers. Less than 1% of the world's water is accessible surface water (rivers, lakes) and groundwater available for human consumption.

Sources of Water Pollution (Point vs. Non-Point) 10 Marks PYQ

Water pollution occurs when toxic substances enter water bodies such as lakes, rivers, and oceans.

  • Point Source Pollution: This originates from a single, discrete, and easily identifiable location. Examples include a discharge pipe from an industrial factory, a municipal sewage treatment plant effluent pipe, or an oil spill from a tanker. Because the source is exact, point-source pollution is easier to monitor, regulate, and control legally.
  • Non-Point Source Pollution: This is diffuse pollution that does not originate from a single discrete source. It is usually caused by rainfall or snowmelt moving over and through the ground. As the runoff moves, it picks up and carries away natural and human-made pollutants. Examples include agricultural runoff (carrying fertilizers and pesticides), urban street runoff (carrying oil and trash), and sediment from construction sites. It is much harder to control.

Various Pollutants and their Toxic Effect on Aquatic Organisms 10 Marks PYQ

  • Excess Nutrients (Nitrates & Phosphates): From agricultural fertilizer runoff. They cause Eutrophication—an explosive overgrowth of algae (algal bloom). When the algae die, decomposing bacteria consume all the dissolved oxygen in the water, creating hypoxic "dead zones" where all fish and aquatic life suffocate and die.
  • Heavy Metals (Lead, Mercury, Arsenic): From industrial discharge. These metals bioaccumulate in fish tissues, causing neurological and reproductive failure.
  • Thermal Pollution: Power plants discharge hot water into rivers. Hot water holds less dissolved oxygen and disrupts the breeding cycles and metabolism of temperature-sensitive fish.
  • Organic Waste (Sewage): Introduces deadly pathogens and increases the biochemical oxygen demand (BOD).

Portability of Water, Municipal Water Supply, Disinfection 3 Marks PYQ

Potability: Refers to water that is safe for human consumption. It must be clear, colorless, odorless, free of toxic chemicals, and entirely free of pathogenic bacteria.

Municipal Water Supply & Disinfection: Municipalities treat raw river/lake water before distributing it. The final and most crucial step is Disinfection, which kills disease-causing microorganisms. The most common method is Chlorination, though UV radiation and Ozonation are also used as modern alternatives.

Primary and Secondary Waste Water Treatment 10 Marks PYQ

Waste water (sewage) must be treated before being discharged into nature to prevent severe pollution.

  • Primary Treatment (Physical Process): The goal is to remove solid, settleable matter.
    • Screening: Sewage passes through bar screens to remove large debris like plastics, rags, and sticks.
    • Grit Removal: Heavy inorganic particles like sand and gravel settle out in a grit chamber.
    • Primary Sedimentation: The water sits in large tanks. Suspended organic solids settle to the bottom as primary sludge, while oils and grease float to the top and are skimmed off.
  • Secondary Treatment (Biological Process): The goal is to remove dissolved and suspended organic matter using microorganisms.
    • Aeration Tank (Activated Sludge Process): The effluent from primary treatment is pumped into tanks and heavily aerated (supplied with oxygen). Aerobic bacteria thrive and rapidly consume the organic waste.
    • Secondary Clarifier: The water flows into a settling tank where the bacteria clump together and settle to the bottom as activated sludge. The clear water at the top is mostly free of organic pollutants and, after disinfection, can be safely discharged into rivers.

BOD and COD Measurement 5 Marks PYQ

These are critical metrics used to measure the level of organic pollution in water.

  • BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand): The amount of dissolved oxygen required by aerobic bacteria to break down the biodegradable organic material in a given water sample over 5 days at 20°C. A high BOD means the water is highly polluted with sewage.
  • COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand): The amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize all organic matter (both biodegradable and non-biodegradable) and oxidizable inorganic chemicals. COD is always a faster test and yields a higher value than BOD.

Rain Water Harvesting 10 Marks PYQ

Definition: Rain water harvesting is the deliberate collection, storage, and purification of rainwater that runs off from rooftops, parks, and roads for direct future use or to recharge groundwater.

Significance:

  • Water Scarcity Solution: Provides an independent water supply during dry seasons and droughts.
  • Groundwater Recharge: Directing rainwater into recharge pits helps replenish rapidly depleting underground aquifers and raises the water table.
  • Flood Control: By capturing runoff locally, it significantly reduces the volume of water flowing into municipal storm drains, preventing urban flooding.
  • Soil Erosion: Reduces surface runoff velocity, thereby preventing the erosion of topsoil.

Watershed Management 10 Marks PYQ

Definition: A watershed (or drainage basin) is an area of land that channels all rainfall and snowmelt to a single common outlet, such as a river mouth or a lake. Watershed management is the comprehensive conservation and rational utilization of land, water, and plant resources within this basin.

Significance and Strategies:

  • It aims to halt ecological degradation and improve the livelihood of the local community.
  • Afforestation: Planting trees across the watershed binds the soil, prevents erosion, and increases the percolation of rainwater into the ground.
  • Check Dams: Building small barriers across streams to slow down water flow, trap sediment, and promote groundwater recharge.
  • Sustainable Agriculture: Encouraging farming practices that do not use excessive chemical fertilizers, preventing non-point source pollution from running into the main river.

Water (Pollution and Control) Act, 1974 10 Marks PYQ

This was the first comprehensive environmental legislation enacted in India.

  • Objective: To provide for the prevention and control of water pollution, and to maintain or restore the wholesomeness of water bodies across the country.
  • Key Provisions:
    • It established the powerful Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs).
    • It strictly prohibits any person or industry from knowingly discharging poisonous, noxious, or polluting matter into any stream, well, or sewer beyond the standards laid down by the Boards.
    • Industries must obtain a "Consent to Establish" and "Consent to Operate" from the SPCB before starting operations.
    • The Boards have the power to inspect facilities, take water samples, and enforce harsh penalties, including shutting down the industry or imprisonment, for violations.
